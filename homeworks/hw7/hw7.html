Bayesian Inference &
Modes of Convergence

Prior · Likelihood · Posterior · LLN · CLT

Two connected investigations: how Bayes' theorem updates beliefs using evidence, and how random variables converge under different mathematical criteria.

01 / BAYESIAN THEORY

Learning from Evidence

Bayes' theorem follows from conditional probability. In statistical inference, it combines what was believed before seeing observations with how plausible those observations are under different hypotheses.

01 · PRIOR

P(H)

Belief about hypothesis H before observing new evidence. Its choice matters most when data are scarce.

02 · LIKELIHOOD

P(E | H)

Probability of observing evidence E if H were true. As a function of a parameter, it is not a probability distribution over that parameter.

03 · POSTERIOR

P(H | E)

Updated belief after combining the prior and likelihood, normalized by the evidence probability P(E).

P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E)

Here P(E) = P(E|H)P(H) + P(E|¬H)P(¬H). The denominator ensures that posterior probabilities sum to one. When H is a model parameter θ, the same principle becomes posterior density ∝ likelihood × prior density.

Conditional probability describes events inside a probability model; Bayesian statistical inference also treats unknown hypotheses or parameters probabilistically and updates their distributions using data.

04 / FOUR MODES OF CONVERGENCE

What Does It Mean for Random Variables to Converge?

These concepts examine different aspects of a sequence Xn and a candidate limit X. They are not interchangeable.

01 · WEAK LLN

In probability

P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0

For every ε > 0, the chance of a noticeable error goes to zero. It does not require almost every sample path to converge.

02 · STRONG LLN

Almost surely

P(lim Xₙ = X) = 1

Outside a probability-zero exceptional set, the entire numerical sequence converges along each sample path.

03 · MEAN SQUARE

In L²

E[(Xₙ − X)²] → 0

The expected squared error tends to zero. This is particularly useful for mean-square error, variance and estimation.

04 · CLT

In distribution

Fₓₙ(t) → Fₓ(t)

The distributions approach one another at continuity points of the limit CDF. Individual outcomes need not become close.

L² convergence ⇒ convergence in probability ⇒ convergence in distribution
Almost-sure convergence ⇒ convergence in probability ⇒ convergence in distribution

Important: almost-sure and L² convergence do not imply each other without extra assumptions. Reverse implications usually fail. Convergence in distribution alone does not imply convergence in probability to a nonconstant random variable.

L², but not almost surely

Moving intervals: Xn indicates a dyadic interval of width 2−k. Every point is hit once per level, but P(Xn=1) → 0. Thus Xn → 0 in L² and probability, not almost surely.

Almost surely, but not L²

If U ~ Uniform(0,1) and Xn = n·1{U ≤ 1/n}, then Xn → 0 almost surely, but E[Xn²] = n → ∞.

In distribution, but not in probability

Let X be equally likely −1 or +1 and define Xn = −X. Every Xn has the same distribution as X, but |Xn − X| = 2 always.

05 / COUNTEREXAMPLE VISUALIZATION

Why Probability Convergence Need Not Be Almost Sure

Choose a point ω in [0,1). At dyadic level k, divide [0,1) into 2k intervals, then visit each interval once. The indicator is 1 when the moving interval contains ω. The event becomes rarer for a random ω, yet each fixed ω is hit once at every level.

06 / LLN AND CLT SIMULATION

The Mean Stabilizes; Its Rescaled Error Remains Random

Simulate independent Bernoulli(p) outcomes. The LLN concerns the sample mean approaching p; the CLT concerns the distribution of its standardized fluctuations.

WEAK LAW

Convergence in probability

The sample mean approaches p; for independent observations, its L² error is p(1−p)/n.

STRONG LAW

Almost-sure convergence

For an infinite iid Bernoulli sequence, the sample mean converges to p with probability one.

CENTRAL LIMIT THEOREM

Convergence in distribution

For 0 < p < 1, Zₙ = √n( X̄ₙ − p ) / √[p(1−p)] approaches N(0,1) in distribution.

07 / WHY √n APPEARS

Variance Additivity Explains the Scaling

For independent, identically distributed variables with finite variance σ², the variance of a sum is nσ² while the variance of an average is σ²/n. Standard deviations therefore scale as √n and 1/√n.

Var(Σ Xᵢ) = nσ²   ⇒   SD(Σ Xᵢ) = σ√n
Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²/n   ⇒   SD(X̄ₙ) = σ/√n

Multiplying the error X̄n − μ by √n prevents its variance from collapsing to zero. After dividing by σ, the classical iid finite-variance CLT gives convergence in distribution to the standard normal. This explains the characteristic √n in confidence intervals, standard errors and asymptotic inference.

Independence matters: in general Var(Σ Xi) = Σ Var(Xi) + 2Σi<j Cov(Xi,Xj). If all observations are the same random variable, there are n² covariance terms in total and the variance of the average need not decrease at all.

Weak LLN: Chebyshev's inequality gives P(|X̄n − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²) for iid observations with finite variance. Strong LLN: iid variables with E|X1| < ∞ satisfy almost-sure convergence. CLT: iid variables with 0 < σ² < ∞ satisfy √n(X̄n − μ)/σ ⇒ N(0,1). The CLT does not claim that the unscaled mean converges to a normal distribution.

08 / CRITICAL DISCUSSION

What These Results Do — and Do Not — Tell Us

Bayesian advantages: prior knowledge can be represented explicitly, evidence can be incorporated sequentially, and posterior probabilities are directly interpretable under the model. Limitations: poorly calibrated likelihoods, subjective or weakly supported priors, selection bias and dependent evidence can distort conclusions. With scarce data, different priors can generate very different posteriors.

Convergence and inference: consistency of an estimator can be established through LLN-type reasoning; the CLT instead describes approximate uncertainty at finite sample sizes. The four modes answer different questions and should not be substituted for one another. A simulation can illustrate a theorem but cannot prove an almost-sure statement, which concerns an infinite sequence.

Cybersecurity relevance: a rare attack can have a low posterior probability even after an alert, when false positives are common. Meanwhile, LLN and CLT can support the estimation and uncertainty analysis of incident rates, provided the underlying assumptions are appropriate.
